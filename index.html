NOTE 01 · RAG

Why grounding beats bigger models in finance

Read note

When I worked on retrieval across financial documents, research reports, and compliance policies, model size was not the main constraint. Retrieval quality was. A fluent answer built on the wrong policy section is still wrong—and in a regulated environment, confidence without provenance creates risk.

I learned to treat retrieval as an engineering system rather than a prelude to generation. That means understanding the document structure, testing chunk boundaries, validating metadata, and measuring whether the right evidence appears before the answer is composed. FAISS and Hugging Face helped with the mechanics, but the important design choice was strict grounding: the system had to stay close to retrieved evidence instead of filling gaps with plausible language.

Auditability also changed how teams evaluated the platform. Compliance users did not need a mysterious “smart” model; they needed to see why an answer was produced and where it came from. Retrieval relevance improved by 38%, but the more durable gain was trust. In finance, I would choose a smaller model attached to a well-tested retrieval layer and a clear audit trail over a larger model that cannot explain its evidence.

NOTE 02 · DATA

The unglamorous fundamentals of reliable pipelines

Read note

The most important parts of a production pipeline rarely make the architecture diagram. They are the checks that catch a column arriving with the wrong type, the validation that identifies an unexpected null spike, and the lineage that tells you which report will break if a transformation changes.

I build pipelines with those questions in mind from the start. In practice, this means profiling data before trusting it, defining checks at handoff points, and making failures visible enough that the right owner can act. SQL and PySpark are powerful transformation tools, but reliability comes from the contracts around those transformations: expected schemas, quality thresholds, metadata, and traceable dependencies.

This work can look slower than simply moving data from source to target. It is usually faster over the full life of a system. At Morgan Stanley, profiling and validation improved downstream model precision by 31%, while curated datasets reduced manual data-preparation effort by 42%. Those outcomes came from making quality part of the pipeline itself. My rule is simple: if a dataset drives a model, dashboard, or business decision, validation and lineage are features—not cleanup tasks.

NOTE 03 · TRUST

What I check before trusting an AI-generated analysis

Read note

I start by separating a well-written answer from a correct analysis. The first check is the query: does the generated SQL join the intended entities, use the right grain, and treat dates, nulls, and duplicates correctly? Then I inspect the assumptions around the question. Terms such as “active customer,” “revenue,” or “risk” often have business definitions that the model cannot infer from column names alone.

Next, I look at the source data and filters. I want to know what was excluded, whether the time window is complete, and whether late-arriving records or schema changes affect the result. I compare the output with a known benchmark or a smaller manual calculation. A surprising answer is not automatically wrong, but it needs stronger evidence.

If the same class of error appears repeatedly, I do not patch the prompt or correct one answer. I fix the semantic layer: documented metrics, curated datasets, validated joins, and metadata the retrieval system can use. This is where data engineering and AI reliability meet. Trustworthy analysis depends less on the model sounding certain and more on whether the surrounding system makes definitions, sources, and calculations explicit enough to verify.
